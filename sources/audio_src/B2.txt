N: Welcome back to Workplace Matters. Today I want to look at a development that has changed recruitment in the last decade: online skills tests. Whether you are applying for a job or hiring staff, you are likely to encounter them.
N: Let's start with why employers like them. A traditional interview is subjective. Research shows that interviewers often decide in the first few minutes, and that decision tends to rest on appearance and personality rather than ability. A standardised test, by contrast, measures every candidate against the same yardstick. It is also efficient: a company that receives two thousand applications for twenty places simply cannot interview everyone.
N: There are different kinds of tests. Language tests, such as those used to assess English, are often adaptive. That means the difficulty changes according to your answers: get a question right and the next one is harder; get it wrong and it becomes easier. The advantage is that the test pins down your level with fewer questions, usually in under an hour, rather than the two or three hours a fixed test would require. Other tests assess numerical reasoning, or ask candidates to complete a realistic task, such as drafting a reply to a customer complaint.
N: But there are drawbacks. The first is anxiety. Many candidates freeze in front of a timer and perform below their real level, especially if the test is the first stage and they feel there is no room for error. The second is fairness. If a test is taken at home, how can the employer be sure that the candidate is the one answering? Some companies use webcams and software that detects suspicious behaviour, but these systems raise privacy concerns, and critics say they flag nervous people unjustly.
N: A third problem is bias. A test that includes cultural references, or an unusual idiom, can disadvantage candidates from different backgrounds even when the skill being measured has nothing to do with them. Good test designers check each question for this, but not every test is designed with that care.
N: So what should candidates do? Firstly, find out the format in advance. Most providers publish sample questions, and practising them is the single most effective thing you can do, not because it makes you more knowledgeable, but because it removes surprise. Secondly, manage your time: if an item is taking too long, make your best guess and move on, since unanswered questions usually count as errors. Thirdly, in an adaptive test, don't be discouraged when questions become difficult. That's a sign that you're doing well.
N: And for employers? Use tests as one tool among several, not the only one. A low score should prompt a conversation rather than an automatic rejection, particularly when the candidate has strong experience. Next week, we'll be discussing salary negotiation. Until then, thanks for listening.
