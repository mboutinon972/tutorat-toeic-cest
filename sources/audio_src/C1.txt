AUDIO Cours : pourquoi les projets de changement échouent | N=am_michael | 1.05
N: Good afternoon, everyone. Today's lecture addresses a question that has occupied management researchers for decades: why do so many organisational change programmes fail? The figure most often quoted is that around seventy percent do not achieve their goals. That number is itself contested, because it is rarely defined what counts as failure, but few scholars dispute that the success rate is disappointing.
N: Let me begin with the explanation that you'll hear most often from executives: resistance to change. Employees, we're told, are set in their ways and cling to routine. This explanation is comfortable for those at the top, because it locates the problem in other people. But the evidence suggests it is, at best, incomplete. When researchers actually interview employees who have opposed a change, they frequently find that the objections were reasonable: the staff had spotted a practical flaw that the planners had overlooked, or they had understood that the new process would add work without adding value.
N: A second cause is poor communication, but not in the way people usually mean. The typical mistake isn't saying too little; it's saying the wrong thing. Leaders tend to explain why the change is good for the organisation, while employees want to know what it means for them: will my job change, will I need new skills, will my team survive? If those questions go unanswered, rumours fill the gap, and rumours are almost always more pessimistic than the truth.
N: Third, there's what Professor Kotter famously called the failure to create urgency. People won't invest effort in a change they don't think is necessary. If a company is doing reasonably well, it's hard to persuade anyone that a transformation is needed. Interestingly, some successful turnarounds begin with leaders deliberately sharing uncomfortable data, such as customer defection rates, to make the problem tangible.
N: Fourth, and I'd argue most underrated, is change fatigue. Many organisations launch initiatives in quick succession, so that employees are still absorbing one programme when the next arrives. Staff learn, rationally, that if they wait, the initiative will fade and be replaced. This produces a polite form of non-cooperation: nobody openly opposes the change, but nobody commits to it either.
N: So what do successful organisations do differently? Studies point to three features. First, they involve people in design early on, not simply in implementation. Those who help shape a change are far likelier to defend it. Second, they limit the number of simultaneous initiatives, and they say publicly what they are going to stop doing, not just what they are going to start. Third, they measure behaviour, not attendance at training sessions. A completed training course tells you very little about whether anyone is working differently.
N: Let me add one caveat. None of this is a recipe. Context matters enormously, and what worked in a small, young technology firm may backfire in a hospital with a strong professional culture. The research gives us better questions to ask rather than guaranteed answers.
N: For next week, please read the two case studies on the course page, and come prepared to discuss which of the four causes we've covered best explains each failure. Thank you.
